A lesson that watches what you do, and teaches back

One concept throughout: Ambition's own RAG lesson, "What RAG adds to an LLM call".

1Thesis

Ambition already asks learners to predict, then throws the answer away. Make every touch on the lesson count as evidence, let a small model read it, let a policy pick the next screen, and judge everything by one thing: a new question answered on her own, days later.

Today the RAG lesson shows three passages and asks "which would you send?". Passage A is a trap: same policy, same words, wrong dates. Picking A suggests she believes "similar text is evidence", the idea lesson 3 ("Find evidence, not just similar text") exists to fix. That pick is never recorded. In the browser's network log, clicking a passage sent no request to Ambition's API; only "viewed this step" was sent. (I did not check third-party analytics, so I'd confirm with the team.)

The design keeps one clear split. Code owns every number, every right/wrong, and every instant response. Small ML models read the learner and choose the next move. An LLM only writes Waymond's 1-2 sentences. That split is what makes it fast, correct, and trainable.

2One learner story

Priya opens the RAG pursuit. The lesson plays like a video, with chapters on a timeline. At the first ◆ it stops: "Drag the passage you would send."

  1. She drags A. Within a second the screen puts her dates beside the question's dates: before 1 June vs on or after 1 June.
  2. Behind the scenes the habit detector moves to "similar = evidence: 80%" and the policy picks "contrast the dates", saving the odds it chose with.
  3. Waymond adds one line, 1-3 seconds later. She highlights "on or after 1 June" as a Question and asks why A looked right.
  4. She tries again, picks B, then clicks Go deeper. A 3D view of the whole pipeline opens; she clicks Retrieve and watches the lesson's own word-counting code pick the wrong passage when the question is reworded.
  5. Three days later, one notification, one new question on a different topic. She picks the real rule, not the word trap. That answer is the reward.

Ravi · engineerPicks B in 4 seconds. Gets less: skips the explanation, goes to a harder reworded question and the code.
Sam · leaves halfwayQuits at chapter 2. Two days later: one 60-second question, resuming exactly where he left.

3.3 · What we save (the trajectory)

One timeline per learner, four kinds of entries:

Every teaching move also saves a receipt: what the model believed, the options it had, the odds of each, and what it chose. That receipt is what lets us test a new policy on old data later. A rewind alone means little (in MOOC videos, 61% of interaction peaks in a sample of 80 videos lined up with visual transitions, Kim et al. 2014), so signals are always read together.

3.4 · The reward

Reward = right on a new question, on her own, 2-7 days later. Nothing else trains the policy.
  • Never rewarded: watch time, clicks, completion, answers while the lesson is still on screen. Plain AI access raised practice scores 48%, but exam scores fell 17% once it was taken away (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025).
  • Hard to game: a separate scheduler picks the day-3 question at random, so the teaching policy cannot pick an easy one. Only her first attempt counts.
  • People who don't return count as missing, not as a pass. We report both "learned" and "came back".
  • Two memories: her own memory (what she asked, got wrong, likes) shapes only her lessons. The shared model learns patterns across everyone.

How it trains: a habit detector (Bayesian update), a skill model (logistic regression, later a transformer), a memory model (per-person forgetting, like Duolingo's half-life regression), and a chooser (rules plus Thompson sampling, later offline RL). 3.5 · Before there are real learners

  1. Authored environments. We write one lesson as a recipe: question, right passage, look-alike trap, off-topic passage. AI writes hundreds of variants; code rejects any where the trap isn't really wrong.
  2. Simulated learners. Small programs with one habit each (falls for look-alikes, checks dates, skims, random, quits), plus how they react to each tutor move, plus noise and forgetting. Written as code, because AI-played students tend to quietly fix their own mistakes (Do, Sonkar, Sachan 2026). AI only writes their chat questions.
  3. Offline evaluation. Every new AI version must pass on the fake students: spots each habit, never leaks the answer, picks sensible moves, doesn't slow down people who already know it. Later the same checks run on saved real sessions.
  4. Human studies. 5 think-alouds × 3 rounds, then 60 paid learners, adaptive vs normal, with a new question 3-5 days later (inspired by Google's Learn Your Way study, which used a 3-5 day retention test: 78% vs 67% for an AI textbook vs a standard PDF reader).
  5. Fix the fakes. Compare fake and real behavior; keep the fakes that match, fix the rest (Waymo's simulation challenge scores fake drivers by how well they match real logged driving), and repeat.

5How we test it

  • Before people: the offline checks above, on every change.
  • Is it usable: 3 rounds of 5 think-alouds. Fix anything confusing.
  • Does it teach: 60 paid learners, new to RAG, randomly split: today's lesson vs the adaptive one. Same day-3 question for both, picked at random.
  • Main measure: share of people who answer the new question right, unaided, 3-5 days later.
  • Also watched: time spent, how many come back, how often Waymond was wrong, whether experts felt slowed down.
  • Decision rule: 60 people is a pilot. If adaptive looks clearly ahead on day 3 without more people quitting, we run a bigger test; if not, we change one thing and rerun.

6The first 6-8 weeks

Week 1
Instrument the RAG lesson: log passage picks, highlights, Ask, skips, rewinds. Write the lesson recipe and 4-5 habits.
Week 2
Fake students and the offline checks. Make 50 checked lesson variants.
Week 3
Think-alouds (5 people × 3 rounds). Fix the screen.
Week 4
Adaptive v1: habit detector, rules + one randomized decision with logged odds, day-3 scheduler, Waymond's short lines.
Weeks 5-6
60-person pilot, adaptive vs normal, check-ins 3-5 days later.
Week 7
Analyze. Tune the fake students against the real sessions.
Week 8
Decide: ship to a small share of learners and switch on Thompson sampling, or change one thing and rerun.

Not in v1: transformer models, offline RL, generated 3D scenes. They come only after the data exists and they beat the simple version on day 3.
